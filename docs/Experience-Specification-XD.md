# Hason — Experience Specification (XD)

This document describes how the platform should **feel**, moment by moment — not how it looks. It sits above the UX spec and the visual language: those decide screens and pixels; this decides emotion. It inherits Master-Direction.md fully. When a design or UX choice is in doubt, it must be resolved in favour of the feeling described here.

## The throughline

One sentence carries the whole product: **"I want to be there" → "This is mine" → "I belong here" → "I was there, and it mattered."**

A participant should feel they have arrived at a serious, human, well-run conference **before they physically arrive** — and leave feeling it stayed with them. Everything below is in service of that arc.

## The governing feelings

Three feelings must be true on every screen, for every role:

- **Calm.** The interface never raises the participant's pulse. Scarcity, deadlines, and errors are real, but they are delivered with grace, never alarm. A person should leave a session with the platform *less* tired, not more.
- **Certainty.** At every step the person knows where they are, what just happened, and what's next — without reading instructions. Confidence is the product's core emotion, especially at the door.
- **Belonging.** From the first confirmation, the participant is a named guest, not a record. The system speaks *to them*, about *their* day, *their* people, *their* place.

## The moments

### 1. Arrival at the site — the first three seconds
Before a word is read, the person should feel *place*: a photograph or a calm editorial hero, generous space, one confident line of type. The feeling is walking into a museum lobby, not opening an app. Within three seconds they know: this is a real event, it is for me, and it is handled with care. No cookie wall, no carousel, no noise competing for the eye.

### 2. Understanding why it matters
Scrolling is not navigation — it is a story. Purpose, then the shape of the days, then the people, then the place. Each beat answers one quiet question ("why should I come?", "can I picture my day?", "do I trust the people behind this?"). The person should feel *invited*, never *sold to*. Registration is the emotional conclusion, never the first ask.

### 3. Deciding to register
When the moment comes, it feels inevitable and safe. One confident action, reassurance beside it ("a few details and your place is saved"). No pressure, no fake urgency. The person feels they are *joining*, not *filling a form*.

### 4. Registration — step one (who you are)
Calm, short, human. Labels in plain language, one column, comfortable rhythm. Every field feels like a courtesy, not an interrogation. Validation arrives gently, on leaving a field, phrased as help ("check the email") never as failure. Progress is visible and honest ("step 1 of 2"). The person never fears losing what they typed — the draft is safe.

### 5. Registration — step two (choosing workshops)
This is where anxiety usually lives, and where grace matters most. Availability is legible at a glance through the client's own language — **פנויה / כמעט מלאה / מלאה / רשימת המתנה** — as calm status, never blinking counters. "Almost full" gently encourages without panic; "full" offers the waiting list as a warm alternative, not a dead end. Choosing feels like composing a personal day, not competing for slots. A double-booking is prevented quietly, explained kindly.

### 6. Confirmation — the exhale
The moment after submitting is reassurance, not a receipt. A warm sentence ("your place is saved"), the next steps stated plainly, and a clear door into the personal area — where the participant is *already signed in*, never bounced to a wall. The QR/entrance code is present and calm ("this works offline too"). The person feels *held*.

### 7. The days before — anticipation
The personal area is a home that fills up as the event nears. A countdown that reads as excitement, not a timer. Reminders that feel personal and few — a whisper the evening before, not a stream. Any change (a room moved, a session added) arrives as a calm, human line, not a red badge. The feeling is a good host keeping you gently informed.

### 8. Arrival and check-in — the threshold
The highest-confidence moment in the product. The participant opens their code; it is large, certain, and works without signal. For the organizer at the door, the scan resolves instantly to a human: name, status, ticket, which workshops. A re-scan is flagged, never blocked — a person decides, the machine advises. The feeling on both sides is *effortless certainty*. Crossing the threshold should feel like being expected.

### 9. During the conference — my day, my people
The live experience is personal and quiet. My schedule reads as a walking path through the day, my chosen sessions marked with one restrained accent, breaks receding. My people are faces with one human reason to meet, never business cards. Updates are timely and few. The participant feels *oriented and unhurried* inside a busy day.

### 10. After — closure and memory
An ending, not an archive. The threshold returns; resources and the certificate wait as calm rows; a certificate is a confident artefact, not a form output. The last feeling is that the event *stayed with them* — and that returning next year is obvious.

## Cross-cutting feelings

- **Transitions between screens** maintain spatial continuity — you move *through* the product, never *between* pages. Motion is almost invisible: a soft settle on enter, nothing that performs. Reduced motion collapses it without loss.
- **Notifications** are a whisper, not an alarm. They surface only what changes a decision. Silence is a feature; an over-notified participant is a failed one. (An activity timeline, when present, reads as a calm log of one's own journey, not a feed demanding attention.)
- **Loading** is a held breath, not a spinner. The shape of what's coming appears (a skeleton), so the wait feels like arrival, not delay.
- **Errors** are a guide, not a wall. They say what happened and the way forward, in the product's calm voice — "registration is closed" with a next step, never a raw failure or a dead 404.
- **Empty states** are invitations, not apologies. No screen is ever blank; each teaches or offers the first step.
- **The organizer's feeling** matters too: calm control, not a cockpit. A live dashboard answers "how is it going?" in one glance — who arrived, which rooms are filling — as editorial answers, never a wall of gauges. The manager should feel *on top of it*, not *drowning in it*.

## The meta-goal

**Make the participant feel they are already at a professional conference — before they arrive.** Every choice is measured against this. If a screen makes the event feel smaller, cheaper, or more administrative than it is, it is wrong, however functional.

## How we know it's working

After building any moment, answer honestly:

- Would a first-time participant feel *welcomed*, or *processed*?
- Does the person always know what just happened and what's next, without thinking?
- Is anything raising the pulse that shouldn't — a counter, a badge, a blunt error?
- Does this moment make the conference feel *more* prestigious and human, or less?
- Would someone remember this experience tomorrow?

If any answer is wrong, the moment is not done — regardless of whether the feature works.
